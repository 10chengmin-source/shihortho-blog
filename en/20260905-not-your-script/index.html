Is Spine Surgery Scary? Someone Else's Story Isn't Your Script

Dr. Shih's Philosophy of Care By Dr. Cheng-Min Shih Published yesterday

In clinic, I often hear two very different voices:

"Doctor, my neighbor couldn't walk after spine surgery — I'm scared to have it done…"

"Doctor, my friend went home the same day after surgery. Can I do that too?"

Someone else's outcome can't be used to predict yours.

"Spine surgery" actually covers an extremely wide range — what each person is dealing with can be completely different.

A simple herniated disc might only need local decompression; spondylolisthesis or instability might need screw fixation and fusion; an osteoporotic compression fracture, under the right conditions, might be treated with a minimally invasive procedure done under local anesthesia — commonly known as "bone cement" vertebroplasty — that lets the patient feel less pain and get moving again more quickly.

But for severe spinal deformity, infection, or a spinal tumor, what's involved may be a much larger, more complex reconstructive surgery.

So when you tell me "my neighbor couldn't walk after surgery," neither of us has actually seen that person's medical records. We don't know their neurological function or overall health before surgery, and we don't know whether they were dealing with a common degenerative condition or something far more complex — a tumor, an infection, or a spinal deformity.

Likewise, a friend going home "the same day" doesn't mean your situation is necessarily the same. Their disease, degree of nerve compression, overall condition, and surgical approach could all be completely different from yours.

What I actually want to understand in clinic is your own situation.

What symptoms you're having right now, how much they're affecting your daily life, whether the physical exam shows any neurological abnormality — putting all of that together with imaging like X-rays and MRI is how I can tell what the real problem is, and what treatment is appropriate right now.

Some people only need observation, rehabilitation, or medication; some are suited to a local injection; only some genuinely need surgery.

Even when it's all called "spine surgery," the scale, risk, and recovery speed can vary enormously.

You can listen to other people's experiences — just don't rush to assume they apply to you.

When you walk into the clinic, what I'm focused on is your spine — not your friend's, and not your neighbor's.
