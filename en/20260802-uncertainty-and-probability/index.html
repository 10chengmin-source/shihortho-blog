Medicine Is a Science Full of Uncertainty, and an Art of Probability

Dr. Shih's Philosophy of Care By Dr. Cheng-Min Shih Published 2026-08-02 Last updated: 2026-08-10 👁 —

In clinic, patients often ask, with real worry, whether skipping surgery would simply be safer. I understand that fear completely: the spine is the core structure supporting the entire body, and anyone would hesitate before operating on it. But in clinical decision-making, understanding risk shouldn't be a one-way street of fear; it should be a genuine weighing of quality of life.

As a spine surgeon, I spend a great deal of time evaluating when surgery is not needed. Patients who know me well know that as long as medication, rehabilitation, or lifestyle adjustments can keep symptoms stable, surgery is never my first choice. I never recommend an operation just because the imaging looks less than ideal. What truly drives my decision is always the patient's actual function and how much their daily life is being disrupted. After a full evaluation, I often tell patients they can go home, observe, and coexist with their symptoms, rather than rushing into invasive treatment.

That said, we also have to confront a different kind of risk: the cost of doing nothing. Many people assume that avoiding surgery is inherently the safer choice, but in clinical practice, prolonged nerve compression can cause permanent loss of muscle strength, unsteady walking, and even a higher risk of falls: functional decline that is often irreversible. Once conservative treatment has reached its limit, the goal of surgery is no longer perfection. It's precisely limiting further loss, before pain and disability squeeze any more space out of daily life. Only once I've confirmed that other approaches are no longer effective do I tell a patient clearly that surgery is currently the only option likely to restore their quality of life.

Medical decisions are never simply black or white, and there's no perfect option. My role isn't to eliminate every uncertainty for the patient. It's to help them clearly see the benefits and risks behind every path. To me, surgery isn't just a technical question; it's about finding, within a controlled level of risk, the option with the least impact on the patient's life now and in the future. What we're pursuing has never been zero risk. It's a precise decision made with full understanding, walking with the patient toward the path most likely to restore their quality of life.
