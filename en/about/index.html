Dr. Cheng-Min Shih

I am Dr. Cheng-Min Shih. My practice spans minimally invasive, complex, and revision spine surgery, hip and knee joint reconstruction, and osteoporosis care.

Current Position

Chief, Division of Spine Surgery, Department of Orthopedics, Taichung Veterans General Hospital.

Education

M.D., Kaohsiung Medical University; Ph.D., National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University.

Training & Experience

Clinical training began at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, followed by a full residency, chief residency, and attending physician appointment in the Department of Orthopedics at Taichung Veterans General Hospital, where he also served as ward chief. He is board-certified in both Orthopedic Surgery and Osteoporosis by Taiwan's national specialty boards. To further refine his technique, he trained at Columbia University Medical Center (thoracolumbar deformity correction), NewYork-Presbyterian Allen Hospital (cervical microsurgery and robotic-assisted spine surgery), and Indiana Orthopedic Hospital (Mazor robotic spine surgery), and was invited as a visiting scholar by the Japanese Society for Spine Surgery and Related Research (JSSR). He trained under Dr. K. Daniel Riew, a leading authority in cervical spine surgery; Dr. Lawrence G. Lenke, a pioneer in spinal deformity correction; and Professor Cheng-Hung Lee, an expert in anterior spine surgery. Together, these three surgeons have each helped define the modern standard for spine surgeons in their respective subspecialties. He is a member of the Taiwan Spine Society, the Taiwan Minimally Invasive Spine Society, and international societies including APSS and AO Spine, and is frequently invited to demonstrate spine surgery both in Taiwan and abroad. He has received multiple institutional awards for clinical excellence, teaching, and research. His research focuses on spinal biomechanics and osteoporosis, with a particular interest in applying artificial intelligence to orthopedic imaging. He also holds a Taiwan patent for a compressible, leak-resistant bone-grafting fusion cage and plate system, recognized with Taiwan's National Innovation Award.

Why I Started This Page

I believe the most important part of a clinic visit isn't rushing to decide on treatment the moment something shows up on imaging — it's first understanding what the patient is actually struggling with.

How symptoms present, how much daily life and function are affected, and whether the physical exam and imaging findings line up with each other all need to be evaluated together. Only once the problem is clarified can I tell whether an abnormality on imaging is really the source of the symptoms — and only then can I judge whether active treatment is needed now, or whether observation, rehabilitation, follow-up, or lifestyle adjustments would be more appropriate first.

That's why I spend a great deal of time in clinic getting to know each patient's condition. Some details that seem very small can end up affecting the overall judgment that follows, so I still try to ask and check everything that needs asking and checking. To me, the outcome of treatment doesn't just depend on which method is ultimately chosen — more importantly, it depends on whether the initial judgment was correct, and whether the right approach was taken at the right time.

Because of this, clinic often runs from morning straight through the evening, and patients or family members often ask whether I've eaten. Really, I just don't want time pressure to mean skipping questions or evaluation that should happen. Thank you to everyone for your patience waiting, and for understanding that clinic sometimes runs longer than expected.

The reason I started this page was to lay out, ahead of time, some of the things I often find myself explaining in clinic.

Clinic time is limited, but there's a lot that needs evaluating and explaining. If you can get a sense of how I approach a visit and some of my basic thinking about illness and treatment before we meet, it's usually easier to get straight to what matters once we do, and it narrows the gap between our expectations.

This page isn't just about sharing medical knowledge — through it, I also hope to give people a little more understanding, and a little less uncertainty, when facing illness and treatment decisions.

If this content can offer a little help when you need to make a decision, I think that's the "Behind the Strength" I hope this page can bring.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does lower back pain with leg numbness always require surgery?

Not always. Whether it's a herniated disc, spinal stenosis, or spondylolisthesis, most cases can first be managed with rehabilitation, posture adjustments, and medication under observation. Surgery is typically recommended only when conservative treatment has limited effect and nerve compression symptoms — such as persistent numbness or weakness in the legs — continue to affect daily life.

Will spine surgery damage nerves or cause paralysis?

This is genuinely one of the biggest worries for most patients. Spine surgery does carry a real risk of nerve injury, but that risk varies a great deal depending on the procedure. For common degenerative spine conditions — such as nerve compression from a herniated disc, spinal stenosis, or spondylolisthesis — serious nerve injury or paralysis after surgery is actually uncommon. For larger procedures such as major spinal deformity correction, spinal tumor removal, or other more complex surgeries, the risk of nerve injury is higher.

Modern surgery also uses tools like intraoperative neuromonitoring and image guidance, which help the surgeon track nerve status and instrument position during the procedure. That said, no surgery can ever be entirely risk-free — the actual risk still needs to be assessed individually based on each patient's condition and the specific procedure involved.

For typical degenerative spine surgery, beyond nerve function, what needs closer attention after the operation is usually whether the wound heals properly and stays free of infection, and — for fusion surgery — whether the bone fuses successfully. This isn't something only the surgeon needs to watch — it also requires the patient's cooperation during recovery.

So the safety of spine surgery isn't just about whether the nerves were injured during the procedure — how well the wound recovers and whether the bone heals properly afterward matter just as much.

What's the difference between minimally invasive and traditional spine surgery?

Minimally invasive spine surgery typically involves smaller incisions and a shorter recovery period, but it isn't suitable for every case. Whether it's appropriate depends on the severity and location of the condition and the patient's overall condition, not simply a preference for a smaller incision.

How do I know whether I should see a spine surgeon or just keep observing?

If you have persistent lower back pain, radiating pain or numbness in the legs, or a noticeably shorter walking distance before symptoms appear, it's worth having an orthopedic or spine surgeon evaluate your imaging and physical exam findings, so you can discuss observation, rehabilitation, or surgery together based on the actual findings.
